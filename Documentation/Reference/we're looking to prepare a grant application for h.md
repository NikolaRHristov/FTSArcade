# we're looking to prepare a grant application for https://frymatic.us/FTSArcade in order to get a grant from https://leverforchange.org/open-calls/boundless-san-francisco/ this is a \$50,000,000 grant which we aim to receive how do we prepare for stage 1

**Prepare Stage 1 as a San Francisco public-benefit proposal that uses FTSArcade as a tool—not simply a request to fund more games. First, confirm that your team registered before September 11: the application deadline was extended to October 15, 2026, at 5 p.m. Pacific Time, but the published registration deadline has already passed.**[^1_1]

My recommendation is to resolve eligibility immediately, then build a focused case around a specific community need, credible local delivery, measurable results, and a justified funding request.

## First: confirm you can apply

As of October 12, you have three days until the published application deadline—but only registered applicants are invited to complete the application.[^1_1]

1. **Find your registration confirmation.** Identify the registered Lead Organization, primary contact, and application invitation.
2. **Open the application and copy its questions.** Record every word limit, attachment requirement, budget field, and submission condition before drafting.
3. **If you did not register, contact the competition immediately.** Ask whether any exception is available at [boundless-sf@submittable.com](mailto:boundless-sf@submittable.com); do not assume the application extension also reopened registration—the published guidance does not say that.[^1_1]
4. **If another organization registered, confirm the permitted arrangement.** Do not assume you can transfer its registration or replace its project without approval.

**This is the first go/no-go decision.** Without a valid route to submit, I would avoid spending the remaining days writing a full application.

## What Stage 1 achieves

Stage 1 is the initial application and review process leading to finalist selection, not an immediate \$50 million award; finalists receive development support before preparing a comprehensive investment prospectus for the final award decision.[^1_1]

The published process includes administrative eligibility review, mandatory peer review, expert evaluation, and finalist selection expected by June 2027, followed by final award selection in fall 2027.[^1_1]

Two implications for your preparation:

- **Make the initial case convincing, but do not confuse it with the later investment prospectus.** Prioritize the actual application questions.
- **Assign someone to the post-submission obligations.** Applicants must review at least four other applications; failing to complete that review can terminate participation.[^1_2]

Also, \*\*\$50 million is the maximum, not a guaranteed award size**: the competition describes final awards between \$10 million and \$50 million.[^1_1]

## Resolve the legal applicant

FTSArcade is the project; you need to identify the legal organization responsible for receiving, controlling, and reporting on the money.

The current rules allow several entity types, including qualifying nonprofits, government entities, and for-profit organizations; individuals and certain single-member LLCs treated as individuals for tax purposes cannot lead an application. [Official rules](https://boundless-sf.leverforchange.org/rules)

For-profit recipients must comply with expenditure-responsibility requirements, and all award spending must serve charitable purposes rather than primarily enriching owners or shareholders. [Official rules](https://boundless-sf.leverforchange.org/rules)

**Prepare this internal eligibility file:**

- Legal name, jurisdiction, entity type, and registration documents
- Tax status and relevant determination documents
- Authorized signatory and governing-body approval
- Recent financial statements and current operating budget
- Proposed project leadership and financial controls
- Ownership or licensing documentation for the platform and its assets

If the proposed applicant is outside the United States, obtain written clarification: the rules list foreign-entity categories, but other wording in the FAQ and definitions refers to U.S.-based organizations. [Official rules](https://boundless-sf.leverforchange.org/rules) [Official FAQ](https://boundless-sf.leverforchange.org/faq)

**A fiscal sponsor or established local lead may be worth considering, but it must genuinely control the project and grant—not merely lend its name.** That control is required by the rules.[^1_2]

## Define the public benefit

The material you shared shows a browser-based arcade with playable games and work-in-progress builds; it does not, by itself, establish San Francisco community outcomes.[^1_3]

My assessment is that the missing grant case is not “more features.” It is **who benefits, what changes for them, and why this platform is a credible way to produce that change**.

Choose one primary purpose rather than combining several loosely related promises:


| Possible direction | Proposed role for FTSArcade | Evidence you would need |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Creative workforce pathways | Entry point into structured game-development training, paid projects, and employment support | Participant learning, completed portfolios, paid placements, employer demand |
| Accessible community learning | Platform for facilitated digital-literacy and creative-technology programs | Demonstrated learning gains, accessibility testing, partner delivery capacity |
| Community connection | Tool for recurring, facilitated activities through neighborhood organizations | Repeat participation, validated belonging measures, evidence beyond website traffic |

These are **possible project designs, not established benefits of FTSArcade**. Choose only a direction your team can support with relevant experience, evidence, and real partnerships.

A useful drafting framework is:

> For **[defined San Francisco population]**, we will address **[documented problem]** through **[specific intervention]**, using FTSArcade for **[defined function]**, delivered with **[confirmed partners]**, to achieve **[measurable outcomes]** over **[time period]**.

The project must primarily benefit people living and working in San Francisco, with no more than 25% of activities aimed at benefiting the broader Bay Area outside city limits.[^1_2]

A globally accessible website alone is therefore not a sufficient local-delivery plan.

## Build around the rubric

The competition evaluates **Transformative Ambition, Viability, Evidence of Results, and Durability**.[^1_1]

Use those criteria as your internal review structure:


| Criterion | What your application should demonstrate | What to prepare |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Transformative Ambition | A meaningful change in opportunity or conditions—not just a larger arcade | Problem statement, theory of change, explanation of what changes structurally |
| Viability | A credible way to deliver in San Francisco | Named leadership, local partners, rollout schedule, staffing and risk plan |
| Evidence of Results | Reasons to believe the intervention can work | Existing results, pilot findings, relevant external evidence, explicit limitations |
| Durability | Benefits that continue beyond the grant | Long-term operating model, institutional adoption, ownership and maintenance arrangements |

The headings above are official criteria; the preparation suggestions are my recommendations.[^1_1]

For an early-stage project, the competition allows relevant analogous evidence and a well-reasoned theory of change where direct results are limited. Nevertheless, do not treat game engagement as proof of educational, employment, or wellbeing outcomes.[^1_1]

**Separate three kinds of claims throughout the application:** what you have already achieved, what external evidence suggests, and what you propose to test.

## Justify the funding request

**Do not start with \$50 million and invent enough activities to spend it.** Build the intervention and calculate what it genuinely requires.

Prepare a five-year planning budget with:

- Community delivery and local partner costs
- Participant support or compensation, where appropriate
- Program staff and management
- Technology development, accessibility, security, and maintenance
- Independent evaluation and data collection
- Legal, safeguarding, insurance, and financial administration
- Indirect costs, with a clearly explained allocation method

The rules permit indirect costs up to 29% of direct project or activity costs, within the award limit—not 29% of the total award. [Official rules](https://boundless-sf.leverforchange.org/rules)

For each major line, document the quantity, unit cost, source of estimate, timing, and intended result. For a \$50 million request, I would also prepare lower-cost scenarios and explain what would be lost at smaller funding levels.

**The central question is:** what verified scale of public benefit makes this amount proportionate?

## Assemble your working packet

This is my recommended preparation packet, **not a verified list of required portal attachments**. I have not obtained your actual application form, so its questions and limits must govern the final submission.

1. **Project brief:** Problem, beneficiaries, intervention, funding request, and expected outcomes.
2. **Local-needs evidence:** Current San Francisco data and documented community input.
3. **Theory of change:** Activities → immediate outputs → participant outcomes → lasting change.
4. **Delivery plan:** Phases, responsibilities, partners, dependencies, and decision gates.
5. **Evidence register:** Every material claim, supporting source, limitation, and evidence gap.
6. **Budget workbook:** Costs, assumptions, annual spending, and sustainability.
7. **Organizational-capacity file:** Leadership experience, finances, governance, and controls.
8. **Risk register:** Adoption, accessibility, safeguarding, privacy, implementation, and financial risks.
9. **Platform-rights file:** Code, artwork, audio, branding, third-party licenses, and contributor agreements.

The rules require appropriate rights and licenses for third-party material included in the application, making an IP review particularly important for a game platform. [Official rules](https://boundless-sf.leverforchange.org/rules)

Seek written confirmation of partner roles now, but distinguish confirmed commitments from discussions. Formal collaboration MOUs are not collected in the initial application, although finalists may be required to provide them. [Official rules](https://boundless-sf.leverforchange.org/rules)

# How do we handle the 501c3 lead organization requirement

**The practical route is to have an eligible existing nonprofit serve as the legal Lead Organization—either as your fiscal sponsor or as the organization running the program with FTSArcade as a delivery partner.** The competition explicitly permits fiscal sponsorship, but the sponsor must genuinely control the grant funds and oversee the project, not simply lend you its 501(c)(3) status.[^2_1][^2_2]

**I also need to correct my earlier advice:** the published eligibility list does not clearly authorize an ordinary for-profit company to lead. Although another section discusses for-profit recipients, that inconsistency is not a safe basis for claiming eligibility; obtain written clarification if you want to pursue that route.[^2_1]

## Choose your structure

For FTSArcade, I would consider these two arrangements:


| Arrangement | Legal and operational structure | When I would recommend it |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Fiscal sponsorship** | An eligible nonprofit leads, receives the award, and exercises financial and project oversight; your team delivers the sponsored project under an agreed arrangement. [^2_1] | When your team has a credible charitable program but needs nonprofit infrastructure |
| **Nonprofit-led partnership** | An eligible nonprofit owns responsibility for the program and selects FTSArcade as a technology or implementation partner; the lead can select contractors and other partners. [^2_3] | When an established San Francisco organization already serves your intended beneficiaries |

**My preference for a \$50 million proposal would be a substantive nonprofit-led partnership**, unless you already have a fiscal sponsor with the capacity and willingness to supervise a project of this size. A strong partner should improve the actual program—not just solve the eligibility paperwork.

## What the nonprofit must control

The Lead Organization must have legal responsibility for the funds, reporting obligations, and actual authority to direct and supervise the proposed solution.[^2_1]

That means your agreement should address:

- **Budget authority:** Who approves expenditures, changes, and payments.
- **Program accountability:** Who decides whether activities fulfill the charitable purpose.
- **Staff and contractors:** Who hires, contracts, supervises, and handles conflicts of interest.
- **Reporting:** Who collects evidence and submits financial and outcome reports.
- **Technology and IP:** What the nonprofit can use, for how long, and what happens if your relationship ends.
- **Termination:** How remaining funds, unfinished work, and participant commitments are handled.

These are recommended negotiation points, not a verified list of required application attachments. Have nonprofit counsel review the arrangement before signing.

**Avoid a “receive the money and immediately pass it all to us” arrangement.** That would not establish the discretion, control, and supervision required of the lead.[^2_1]

## Keep the charitable project distinct

I recommend separating **FTSArcade the platform** from **the San Francisco charitable program that uses it**.

For example, a nonprofit might run a community creative-technology program, while your team supplies the arcade platform, training tools, and technical services. This is a proposed structure—not evidence that your current arcade already delivers those outcomes.

Before approaching a partner, prepare clear answers to:

- What specific San Francisco population will benefit?
- What services will participants receive beyond access to games?
- What outcomes will you measure?
- What existing evidence supports the intervention?
- What will the nonprofit control?
- What will your team retain, including existing code and branding?
- Will your team be paid, and how will compensation and licensing costs be justified?

You do **not** need to offer ownership of all your existing IP as an opening position. Instead, propose sufficient, durable usage rights for the charitable program and negotiate the details with the lead and counsel.

## Find an appropriate lead

Two organizations worth investigating—not confirmed partners—are:

- **[Community Initiatives](https://communityinitiatives.org/):** A dedicated fiscal sponsor offering nonprofit infrastructure, including financial management and compliance support.[^2_4]
- **[Intersection for the Arts](https://theintersection.org/get-support/fiscal-sponsorship/):** Offers fiscal sponsorship for arts and culture projects, with stated fit criteria including community-based, noncommercial work in the Bay Area.[^2_5]

Neither should be assumed willing to sponsor FTSArcade, capable of accommodating this particular award, or available before your deadline. Intersection’s published pages also give conflicting application-availability information, so contact it directly rather than relying on an online application status.[^2_6][^2_5]

For the nonprofit-led route, prioritize organizations already delivering the specific service you propose—such as creative education or workforce development—rather than approaching unrelated charities.

**Ask prospective leads these questions immediately:**

1. Does the proposed program fit your mission?
2. Will you accept legal responsibility and actual project oversight?
3. Can you administer a potential multiyear award up to \$50 million?
4. What due diligence and board approval do you require?
5. What fees, staffing costs, and reporting obligations would apply?
6. Can you authorize an application within the available time?

## Resolve registration before committing

**A sponsor does not automatically fix a missed registration deadline.** The competition required registration by September 11, 2026, and its FAQ says fiscal-sponsor information belongs in designated registration and application sections.[^2_3][^2_7]

Before changing the applicant or spending money on sponsorship, ask the competition administrators:

> “FTSArcade is preparing a fiscally sponsored proposal. Our registration status is [state it accurately]. Can an eligible fiscal sponsor be added or substituted as the Lead Organization at this point? Which documents and approvals must be in place before submission?”

Also ask whether the sponsor must already have completed its own registration. **Do not assume a new sponsor can inherit your registration or that an existing registrant can add a new project without approval.**

## Prepare the sponsor packet

I would send a short, decision-ready packet rather than a long pitch:

- Two-page charitable-program description
- Defined San Francisco beneficiaries and delivery partners
- Draft budget, including your team’s proposed payments
- Team biographies and evidence of relevant experience
- Platform ownership and third-party licensing information
- Proposed governance and division of responsibilities
- Exact grant questions, deadline, and registration status

Request the prospective lead’s IRS determination letter, legal name, EIN, public-charity classification, financial information, and written authorization to use its details. Verify the applicable eligibility category: the common public-charity route requires 501(c)(3) status **and** classification under 509(a)(1) or 509(a)(2), while other specified categories also exist.[^2_1]

**The next decision depends on two facts: did you register by September 11, and is FTSArcade currently owned by an individual, a company, or a nonprofit?**

# do we have to open a foundation?

**No—you do not have to create a foundation.** Boundless San Francisco explicitly allows an existing eligible nonprofit to act as the **fiscal sponsor and Lead Organization** for your project.[^3_1]

## Your practical options

| Option | What it means for FTSArcade |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Use a fiscal sponsor** | Your project operates under an eligible nonprofit’s sponsorship; the sponsor controls the grant funds and is named in the grant agreement if you win. [^3_2] |
| **Partner with an existing nonprofit** | The nonprofit leads the charitable program, with your team potentially serving as a technology or implementation partner selected by the lead. [^3_1] |
| **Create your own eligible nonprofit** | For the standard public-charity route, the organization must already have an effective IRS determination letter recognizing 501(c)(3) status and the required public-charity classification; merely registering an organization is insufficient. [^3_1] |

**My recommendation is to pursue an existing nonprofit, not create a foundation solely for this application.** Look for genuine mission alignment, San Francisco delivery capability, and the capacity to oversee a large grant.

## What you cannot shortcut

A sponsor cannot simply lend you its nonprofit status and hand over the money: the lead must exercise actual legal and operational control, supervision, and financial reporting responsibility.[^3_1]

Likewise, **“foundation” is not itself the eligibility requirement**—the rules distinguish specific qualifying categories, including public charities and private operating foundations.[^3_1]

Before incorporating anything or paying a sponsor, ask the competition whether a sponsor can be added to your application at this stage; its FAQ requires sponsor details in designated registration and application fields but does not establish permission to substitute a lead now.[^3_2]

**Did your team register by September 11, and under whose legal name?** That determines the immediate next step—not whether you open a foundation.

**This rubric tells us that FTSArcade needs to be the enabling technology for a credible San Francisco intervention—not the entire grant proposition.** Reviewers will assess structural change, local execution, evidence, and lasting benefits, with each criterion carrying equal weight.[^5_1]

My recommendation is to design the project against those four tests **before** writing persuasive application language.

## What this changes

A proposal to “build more games and make them freely available” would leave important questions unanswered: what conditions change, how the intervention reaches San Franciscans, why it works, and what survives after funding?

Those are central to the rubric, including whether the ambition is proportionate to the amount requested.[^5_1]

Use this as your planning matrix:

| Criterion | Question your team must answer | Recommended supporting material |
|---|---|---|
| Transformative ambition | What barrier or opportunity structure changes—not simply what service grows? | Specific problem, causal explanation, theory of change |
| Viability | Who delivers the program in San Francisco, and how? | Local delivery plan, named responsibilities, partner commitments, realistic costs |
| Evidence of results | Why should reviewers believe this approach produces the intended outcomes? | Direct results where available, relevant analogous evidence, limitations |
| Durability | What benefits and delivery capacity remain after the grant? | Long-term operating costs, institutional adoption, funding and ownership arrangements |

These questions translate the official criteria into a preparation checklist; the suggested materials are my recommendations.[^5_1]

## Choose a defensible intervention

One **possible** direction—not a claim about what FTSArcade already achieves—is a San Francisco creative-technology pathway:

> Use accessible games as an entry point into supported game creation, paid practical projects, and pathways into further education or employment.

In that design, the arcade attracts participation; facilitated learning and paid opportunities are the intervention. The proposed structural change would be reducing barriers to creative-technology opportunities through durable access, paid participation, and institutional pathways.

But **only choose this direction if your team can actually deliver it**. Do not add workforce development language merely because it sounds fundable.

Before committing, establish:

- **Population:** Which San Franciscans, with what unmet need?
- **Barrier:** Cost, equipment access, lack of mentoring, unpaid entry pathways, or something else?
- **Intervention:** What precisely addresses that barrier?
- **Delivery:** Who provides instruction, support, and opportunities?
- **Outcome:** What changes beyond participation and gameplay?
- **Alternative:** Why this approach instead of an existing program?

## Write the causal chain

I would draft a one-page theory of change using this structure:

**Documented barrier → program activity → immediate output → participant outcome → lasting change**

For the hypothetical pathway above:

| Element | Illustrative content—not established results |
|---|---|
| Barrier | Participants face a verified obstacle to accessing creative-technology training |
| Activity | Local partners deliver supported game-building projects and paid practical experience |
| Output | Participants complete projects and receive instruction and mentoring |
| Outcome | Participants demonstrate skills and progress into paid work or further education |
| Lasting change | Institutions maintain accessible entry pathways beyond the grant |

Every arrow is an assumption you must explain and support. For example, completing a game does not automatically lead to employment; the proposal needs a credible mechanism connecting the two.

## Build an honest evidence case

**Early-stage applicants are not automatically ruled out.** The rubric allows a rigorous theory of change supported by strong analogous evidence when direct results are limited.[^5_1]

Separate your evidence into three categories:

- **Product evidence:** Does the platform function reliably and accessibly?
- **Delivery evidence:** Can your team recruit, support, and retain the intended participants?
- **Outcome evidence:** Does the intervention produce the claimed learning, employment, or other benefit?

Website traffic and repeat play may support engagement claims. I would not present them as proof of learning or employment outcomes without additional measurement.

For external evidence, record the studied population, intervention, outcomes, and differences from your proposal. Evidence about structured, instructor-led learning cannot simply be treated as evidence that an unassisted arcade produces the same results.

## Make local execution concrete

An organization without an existing San Francisco presence can still score well, but it needs a compelling, realistic plan for establishing that presence.[^5_1]

For your internal delivery plan, specify:

- Local program leadership and decision-making
- Confirmed versus prospective partners
- Recruitment channels and participant eligibility
- Locations, equipment, accessibility, and support needs
- Staffing and safeguarding responsibilities
- Implementation dependencies and fallback plans
- Community input that will shape the program

**Naming organizations you hope to approach is not the same as securing delivery capacity.** Label those relationships accurately.

## Make $50 million proportionate

The rubric explicitly tests whether the theory of change is proportionate to the resources requested.[^5_1]

My recommendation is to build a bottom-up budget and ask:

1. What outcomes are realistically achievable?
1. What delivery scale is needed to achieve them?
1. What does that scale cost?
1. What portion is technology versus community delivery?
1. What happens if recruitment, adoption, or outcomes fall short?
1. What annual operating costs remain after the grant?

A $50 million request should emerge from that analysis. If the credible program costs substantially less, requesting the maximum could weaken rather than strengthen the application.

For durability, identify who maintains the technology, pays ongoing costs, retains program expertise, and protects participant access. Open-source code or free access could help, but neither alone constitutes a complete operating plan.

## Run a mock review

Have independent readers score each criterion from 1 to 5 and explain their reasoning, mirroring the official review approach.[^5_1]

Ask each reader to identify:

- The strongest substantiated claim
- The most important unsupported assumption
- The main execution risk
- What would justify the next higher score

Also note a small arithmetic inconsistency in the published text: four criteria scored 1–5 produce a **4–20** range per reviewer, not 5–20; the stated maximum of 80 across four reviewers is consistent.[^5_1]

**The next useful step is a two-page project concept—not a full grant narrative.** It should define the San Francisco problem, beneficiaries, intervention, evidence, local delivery, funding logic, and post-grant model. What public-benefit outcome do you actually want FTSArcade to produce: creative skills, paid career pathways, community connection, or something else?

# ```
Lever for Change addresses possible biases in the evaluation process both on the front end, before the scoring begins, and on the back end, after the reviewers have submitted their scores.

On the front end, Lever for Change’s Participatory Review and Evaluation Panel Review webinars review the Competition eligibility criteria and the Evaluation Rubric and includes training on recognizing and addressing the biases that we all bring as we read and assess proposals.

Lever for Change also works to address unconscious bias inherent in the evaluation process.

During both Participatory Review and Evaluation Panel Review, each reviewer only reviews a very small subsample of the entire set of applications. Therefore, each reviewer has a very different reference point by which to adjudicate each application. For example, a reviewer may only see very good applications. Because these applications are of similarly good quality, the reviewer may decide to score these applications lower than they deserve. By contrast, a reviewer that only sees poor applications may give better scores on average. Furthermore, reviewers may have intrinsic cognitive biases. Even under ideal conditions, reviewers are prone to making non-objective judgements about the applications they review, and so Lever for Change normalizes the raw scores provided by our evaluators to reduce the risk that bias in the assessment process eliminates a strong application from consideration.

Normalization relies on finding the mean score given for all applications and the mean score given by each reviewer. Reviewers whose mean score is higher than the global average will have their scores devalued to account for whatever biases led to inflated scores. Similarly, reviewers whose scores are lower than the global average will have their scores boosted to compensate for their tougher-than-average assessments.

The evaluation and normalization process will be conducted at two points during the Competition: first, after the conclusion of Participatory Review and, later, after the conclusion of Evaluation Panel Review. A submission that ranks among the top applications, as determined by Participatory Review scores, will be eligible to advance to Evaluation Panel Review. A submission that ranks among the top applications, as determined by Evaluation Panel Review scores, may be considered for selection as a Finalist.

As a further risk mitigation step, Lever for Change staff review reviewers’ scores and comments for every submission.

If at any point in the process, it is determined that an applicant does not meet the eligibility criteria of the Competition as laid out in the Competition Rules, the applicant will be notified and will not be eligible to progress to further stages of the Competition.

## What normalization means

A high raw score is not necessarily your final comparative result: scores from unusually generous reviewers are adjusted downward, while scores from unusually strict reviewers are adjusted upward.[^6_1]

The explanation does **not** specify the exact formula, advancement cutoff, or number of applications advancing, so we cannot reliably calculate your odds from a hypothetical score. Normalization addresses scoring tendencies; it does not guarantee that every misunderstanding or bias disappears.[^6_1]

## How we should prepare

My recommendation is to make every important claim **clear, supported, and connected to a scoring criterion**.


| Criterion | How to reduce reviewer uncertainty |
| :-- | :-- |
| Transformative ambition | Identify the specific condition you will change and explain why the intervention goes beyond providing games |
| Viability | State who delivers what in San Francisco, with timelines, costs, responsibilities, and accurately described partnerships |

For FTSArcade specifically, I would avoid making reviewers infer the public benefit from the website. The application should explain the complete intervention in plain language, including what happens **beyond playing the games**.

## Test comprehension before scoring

Run a mock review with readers who do not already know the project. Before asking them for scores, ask them to answer:

1. Who in San Francisco benefits?
2. What problem does the project address?
3. What does the team actually deliver?
4. Why should that produce the claimed outcomes?
5. Why is the requested amount justified?
6. What remains after funding ends?

If their answers differ substantially, revise the proposal before polishing its language. Then ask each reader to score the four criteria independently and explain what evidence would justify a higher score.

**Do not normalize your small mock-review sample to predict the official result.** Use disagreements to find unclear claims and weak assumptions instead.

Keep two separate internal checklists:

- **Eligibility:** Legal lead, registration, permitted organizational structure, required approvals, and grant-use compliance
- **Competitiveness:** Local relevance, credible execution, evidence, proportionate budget, and lasting benefits

# exactly, let's formalize the methodology for writing so

The methodology is to build one evidence-backed project case first, then derive both the internal prospectus and the Stage 1 application from it. Do not write promotional prose before the project logic, evidence, budget, and eligibility are settled.

The official application questions are not publicly published, so this is an internal writing system rather than a guaranteed portal template. The application must be in English, complete, and submitted through the registered account by October 15, 2026, at 5 p.m. Pacific Time.[^9_1][^9_2]

## Writing principles

1. **Write one claim at a time.** Every sentence should make a factual, causal, or proposed claim that can be checked.
2. **Label certainty.** Use “demonstrated,” “supported by analogous evidence,” or “proposed assumption.” Never blend them.
3. **Lead with the public benefit.** Introduce FTSArcade only after defining who benefits and what changes.
4. **Match the four criteria.** Each major section must support transformative ambition, viability, evidence, or durability.[^9_3]
5. **Write for an unfamiliar reviewer.** A peer reviewer may know neither games nor your organization.[^9_3]
6. **Keep the requested funding proportionate.** Do not begin with \$50 million and reverse-engineer activities.
7. **Separate legal control from delivery roles.** The Lead Organization must actually control funds and implementation.[^9_1]

## Build the source case

Create one master document before drafting narrative text. Every later prospectus or application paragraph must trace back to it.


| Source section | Required content | Completion test |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Beneficiary | Specific San Francisco population | A reviewer can identify who is included and excluded |
| Problem | Current condition and its cause | Supported by a source or documented observation |
| Intervention | Activities, dose, setting, and duration | Someone else could understand what happens |
| Platform role | What FTSArcade uniquely contributes | Its function is narrower than “the whole project” |
| Theory of change | Barrier, activity, output, outcome, lasting change | Every arrow has evidence or an explicit assumption |
| Delivery | People, partners, locations, timeline, dependencies | Responsibilities are assigned |
| Evidence | Direct results, analogous evidence, gaps | No unsupported effectiveness claim remains |
| Budget | Quantities, unit costs, timing, assumptions | Costs come from the delivery plan |
| Durability | Post-grant owner, funding, access, maintenance | Benefits do not depend only on continued grant funding |
| Eligibility | Lead entity, authority, charitable use, rights | A named person can verify each item |

## Use a fixed paragraph method

For every substantive paragraph, follow this sequence:

1. **State the claim.**
2. **Give the most relevant fact or mechanism.**
3. **Explain why it matters to San Francisco.**
4. **Identify the evidence, limitation, or implementation condition.**
5. **Connect it to the next action or outcome.**

A strong paragraph answers “what, why, how, and on what basis” without adding slogans.

Use this internal drafting formula:

> For [defined beneficiaries], [named barrier] limits [specific opportunity]. The project will [specific activity], delivered by [responsible party], so that [measurable result] occurs. This is expected to contribute to [broader change] because [evidence or reasoned mechanism]. [Named party] will sustain [specific element] after the grant through [mechanism].

Delete any clause that cannot be supported or assigned to someone.

## Draft in this order

### 1. Define the intervention

Write a half-page description containing:

- Who participates
- What they receive
- How often and for how long
- Where it happens
- Who delivers it
- What FTSArcade does
- What success looks like after one year

If this cannot be written clearly, the prospectus is not ready.

### 2. Build the theory of change

Create one row per causal link:


| Link | Statement | Basis | Risk if wrong |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Problem to activity | Why this activity addresses the barrier | Evidence or assumption | Activity may not matter |
| Activity to output | What the activity produces | Delivery plan | Output may not occur |
| Output to outcome | Why the output changes participants’ conditions | Evidence or assumption | Outcome may not follow |
| Outcome to system change | How individual results alter a broader structure | Evidence or assumption | Change remains small or temporary |

Do not claim system change merely because many people receive a service. Explain the institutional, policy, economic, or opportunity-structure mechanism.

### 3. Construct the evidence register

Assign every material claim one status:

- **Demonstrated:** Your team has relevant data.
- **Analogous:** External evidence supports a similar mechanism, with differences disclosed.
- **Assumed:** Plausible but untested; include how and when it will be tested.
- **Removed:** Cannot be supported and is not necessary.

Early-stage applicants may rely on a well-reasoned theory of change and analogous evidence, but weak or absent evidence receives a lower score.[^9_3]

### 4. Build the budget from operations

For each activity, specify quantity, frequency, staffing, partner cost, participant support, technology, evaluation, and administration. Then calculate the total.

Only after that should you test whether the request should be \$10 million, \$50 million, or another amount within the published award range.[^9_3]

### 5. Write the narrative last

Draft the internal prospectus in this order:

1. Problem and beneficiaries
2. Intervention
3. Why it changes conditions
4. San Francisco implementation
5. Evidence and evaluation
6. Budget and proportionality
7. Five-year and post-grant durability
8. Governance, rights, and risk

Each section should be understandable independently because application questions may split the material differently.

## Convert it into the application

Once the portal questions are available, make a conversion matrix:


| Application question | Source sections used | Rubric criteria served | Word limit | Unsupported items removed |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| [Paste exact question] | [Section numbers] | [Relevant criteria] | [Portal limit] | [Claims cut] |

Answer the question directly in the first sentence. Do not begin with organizational history unless the question asks for it.

Then cut in this order:

1. Repeated context
2. Adjectives that make no factual difference
3. Activities not funded or scheduled
4. Claims without evidence
5. Technical detail a reviewer does not need

## Review before submission

Use three separate reviews:

- **Comprehension review:** An unfamiliar reader explains the project without looking back at the draft.
- **Rubric review:** Four readers independently score all four criteria and identify what prevents a higher score.[^9_3]
- **Evidence review:** Another person checks every factual, partnership, budget, legal, and outcome claim against the source case.

A section passes only when the reviewer can identify the beneficiary, action, responsible party, evidence basis, and limitation without inference.

## Operating rules for the writing team

- One person owns the source case; others propose changes rather than creating competing versions.
- No new promise enters the narrative unless it is added to the delivery plan, budget, and evidence register.
- Partnership language must say “confirmed,” “in discussion,” or “proposed.”
- Product metrics such as visits or gameplay cannot be relabeled as learning or employment outcomes.
- The Lead Organization’s authority must be described accurately, especially if a sponsor or partner is involved.[^9_1]
- Everything submitted must be in English.[^9_1]

The immediate deliverable is the source-case table, not prose. Once that is complete, the internal prospectus can be drafted in one pass and then condensed to the portal questions.

# more

The methodology needs a second layer: a claim-control system, section templates, and editing tests that keep the prospectus internally consistent. The goal is to make every sentence traceable to a beneficiary, activity, owner, cost, and evidence status before anyone edits for style.

## Claim ledger

Assign every material statement an ID before using it in prose. No prospectus sentence should introduce a claim that is absent from this ledger.


| ID | Claim | Type | Evidence status | Owner | Budget link | Rubric link | Permitted wording |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| C1 | Who benefits | Factual | Source required | Program lead | Participant costs | Ambition, viability | Name the population precisely |
| C2 | What barrier exists | Factual | Source or documented observation | Research lead | None | Ambition | State scope and limitation |
| C3 | What activity occurs | Operational | Delivery plan | Delivery lead | Activity budget | Viability | State dose, setting, duration |
| C4 | Why activity changes outcome | Causal | Evidence or explicit assumption | Evaluation lead | Evaluation budget | Evidence | Distinguish support from assumption |
| C5 | What remains after funding | Forward-looking | Sustainability plan | Executive owner | Post-grant budget | Durability | Identify mechanism and responsible party |

Use four claim types only:

- **Factual:** Something currently observable or documented.
- **Operational:** Something the project will do.
- **Causal:** Why an activity should produce a result.
- **Forward-looking:** What is expected to continue or change later.

This prevents a sentence such as “the arcade empowers youth” from entering the draft. It has no defined population, mechanism, evidence status, owner, or measurable result.

## Evidence grading

Every causal claim needs a grade before drafting.


| Grade | Meaning | Permitted treatment |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| E1 | Direct evidence from this project and population | State as demonstrated, with metric and limitation |
| E2 | Direct evidence from a closely comparable program | State similarity and relevant differences |
| E3 | Relevant analogous evidence | Use to support the mechanism, not identical outcomes |
| E4 | Plausible but untested assumption | Label as an assumption and include a test |
| E5 | Insufficient support | Remove from the prospectus |

A sentence may move down in confidence, but never up. For example, an E3 finding cannot be rewritten as “proven.” If removing an E5 claim damages the project logic, the project design—not the prose—needs revision.

## Section templates

Write each section to a fixed job. A section fails if it tries to accomplish another section’s job.

### Problem

Include:

- Beneficiary definition
- Current condition
- Scale within San Francisco
- Cause or maintaining barrier
- Consequence of inaction
- Source and limitation

Exclude the solution. If the problem cannot stand independently, the intervention is probably shaping the evidence rather than responding to it.

### Intervention

Specify:

- Participant pathway from recruitment through completion
- Frequency, duration, and setting
- Responsible organization for each step
- What portion is facilitated by people rather than software
- What FTSArcade contributes
- What happens when a participant misses sessions or lacks equipment

A reviewer should be able to reconstruct the participant experience chronologically.

### Structural change

Answer:

- Which system, policy, norm, resource, or opportunity structure changes
- Who controls that structure now
- What leverage the project has over it
- Why individual outcomes accumulate into a broader change
- What result would remain even if one cohort ended

If the only result is that participants received a service, classify it as service delivery and revise the structural claim.

### Delivery

Use one implementation row per workstream:


| Workstream | Output | Responsible party | Dependencies | Start condition | Failure response |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Recruitment | Eligible participants enrolled | Named organization | Access to population | Agreement and staffing | Alternative channel or reduced cohort |
| Program delivery | Sessions completed | Named team | Facilitators and facilities | Training complete | Pause cohort; do not expand |
| Platform | Required functions available | Named technical owner | Licensing and maintenance | Acceptance test passed | Use manual fallback or delay launch |
| Evaluation | Usable outcome data | Named evaluator | Consent and instruments | Baseline defined | Correct method before scaling |

“Proposed,” “in discussion,” and “confirmed” must remain visible in this table and in any prose derived from it.

### Budget narrative

For every major cost, follow this sequence:

1. Name the operating assumption.
2. Show the quantity and unit cost.
3. Connect it to an output.
4. State what happens if the assumption changes.
5. Identify any cost that continues after the grant.

Do not use percentages alone. “Twenty percent for technology” is not an explanation unless the underlying activities and costs are shown.

## Decision gates

The writing process should stop at defined gates rather than proceeding automatically to prose.


| Gate | Required condition | If unmet |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Beneficiary gate | Population, need, and geography are defined | Do not draft the problem |
| Mechanism gate | Every causal arrow is E1–E4 | Remove or redesign unsupported links |
| Delivery gate | Each activity has an owner and dependency | Do not claim viability |
| Budget gate | Request equals supported costs | Resize project or request |
| Eligibility gate | Lead has authority and can lawfully control funds | Resolve structure before submission |
| Translation gate | Every portal answer maps to source claims | Do not submit narrative-only text |

A missed gate cannot be repaired with stronger adjectives, design language, or a longer vision statement.

## Editing tests

Run these tests after the first complete draft:

- **Deletion test:** Remove FTSArcade from the paragraph. If no intelligible intervention remains, the project is underdefined.
- **Substitution test:** Replace the beneficiary with a different city. If no San Francisco-specific facts remain, viability is too generic.
- **Owner test:** Underline every action. Any action without a responsible party is not yet operational.
- **Evidence test:** Underline every causal verb, such as “improves,” “leads to,” or “enables.” Each must point to an evidence grade.
- **Budget test:** Underline every promised output. Each must have a cost or an explicit zero-cost explanation.
- **Durability test:** End the grant on paper. Identify which activities, jobs, maintenance, and participant benefits survive.
- **Proportionality test:** Reduce the request by half. State exactly which outputs disappear and why the original request is consequently justified.
- **Adversarial test:** A reviewer challenges the weakest assumption. The draft should acknowledge and test it before the reviewer discovers it.


## Version control

Use three documents only:

1. **Source case:** Claims, evidence, operations, and costs. This is authoritative.
2. **Internal prospectus:** Long-form explanation derived from the source case.
3. **Application answers:** Portal-specific responses derived from the prospectus.

Changes flow downstream only. If application editing reveals a better idea, add it to the source case first and then revise the prospectus. This prevents unsupported promises from appearing spontaneously in the final answers.

Each version should carry:

- Date and author
- Claims added or retired
- Budget version
- Partnership-status changes
- Unresolved eligibility questions


## Review roles

Separate writing authority from approval authority.


| Role | Responsibility | Cannot do |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Source owner | Maintains claims and consistency | Invent evidence |
| Program lead | Validates intervention and delivery | Expand scope without budget changes |
| Evidence reviewer | Grades claims and identifies limits | Rewrite weaknesses as facts |
| Budget owner | Ties costs to activities | Add narrative commitments |
| Independent reader | Tests clarity and scoring | Repair gaps through interpretation |
| Authorized signatory | Approves final submission | Submit unresolved eligibility claims |

At least one reviewer should be unfamiliar with games and one unfamiliar with the organization. Their misunderstandings are drafting defects, not reviewer failures.

## Final compression

When converting the prospectus into application answers, compress in this order:

1. Preserve beneficiary, intervention, mechanism, evidence, cost, and owner.
2. Remove background that the question does not request.
3. Replace broad verbs with operational verbs.
4. Cut claims graded E5.
5. Reduce repeated justification.
6. Check that the answer still makes sense without adjacent answers.

The finished answer should begin with the conclusion, follow with the mechanism, and end with the condition or limitation. Style editing comes only after this sequence.

The next practical step is to create the claim ledger with roughly 15–25 claims. Once those claims are graded and assigned owners, the prospectus can be assembled without reopening the project concept in every paragraph.

The next layer is a production system: define the analytical work products, set the standard of proof for each, and use explicit rules for converting analysis into prospectus language. This keeps the document from growing while unresolved questions remain.

## Work products

Create the prospectus from completed work products, not from a blank narrative outline.


| Work product | Core question | Minimum acceptable content | Blocks prospectus if missing |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Beneficiary definition | Who is served? | Inclusion rules, exclusion rules, geography, estimated reachable population | Yes |
| Problem analysis | What condition will change? | Baseline, cause, consequence, source, limitation | Yes |
| Intervention specification | What exactly happens? | Pathway, dose, setting, staffing, completion point | Yes |
| Platform specification | What does FTSArcade contribute? | Functions used, users, dependencies, fallback | Yes |
| Theory of change | Why should results follow? | Causal links, assumptions, evidence grades | Yes |
| Delivery map | Who executes? | Responsibilities, sequence, dependencies, partner status | Yes |
| Measurement plan | How will results be known? | Indicators, data source, timing, responsible party | Yes |
| Cost model | What funding is justified? | Quantities, unit costs, timing, sensitivity | Yes |
| Durability model | What survives the grant? | Owner, operating cost, revenue or adoption mechanism | Yes |
| Risk register | What could invalidate the plan? | Risk, likelihood, consequence, mitigation, residual risk | No, but weak viability |
| Rights schedule | Can the lead use the assets? | Owner, license, restrictions, third-party rights | Yes before submission |

Each work product needs an owner and a status: draft, reviewed, or approved. Approved means accepted for use in prose, not necessarily perfect.

## Standards of proof

Use different standards for different kinds of statements.


| Statement type | Required support | Unacceptable support |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Population need | Current source, local observation, or documented partner data | National statistic presented as a local fact |
| Organizational capacity | Named people, relevant experience, available time | General confidence in the team |
| Partnership | Written confirmation or accurately labeled discussion | Logo, informal enthusiasm, or planned outreach |
| Outcome | Relevant evidence or an explicitly testable assumption | Engagement metrics substituted for outcomes |
| Cost | Supplier estimate, historical cost, or stated planning assumption | Round percentage without quantity |
| Structural change | Mechanism showing who changes what | Large participant count alone |
| Sustainability | Named payer, adopter, or operating model | Intention to seek future funding without a pathway |

Where support is incomplete, narrow the statement. “We will test whether facilitated game-building improves completion of creative projects” is admissible as a plan; “the platform transforms employment outcomes” is not admissible without evidence.

## Prospectus architecture

Use the internal prospectus as a decision document. Each section should enable a reviewer to make one judgment.

1. **Decision summary:** Beneficiary, intervention, change sought, request, and result.
2. **Need:** Condition, scale, cause, and consequence.
3. **Solution:** Participant pathway and platform function.
4. **Change mechanism:** Why outputs become outcomes and broader change.
5. **San Francisco execution:** Sequence, partners, capacity, and establishment plan.
6. **Evidence:** What is known, analogous, assumed, and untested.
7. **Measurement:** What will be collected and what decision it supports.
8. **Financial plan:** Costs, request, contingencies, and proportionality.
9. **Durability:** Five-year operation and post-grant continuation.
10. **Governance and risk:** Authority, controls, rights, and failure responses.

The summary should be written last, using only claims already accepted in the other sections.

## Conversion rules

Apply these rules when turning tables into prose:

- One paragraph may contain no more than one causal chain.
- Every number must appear in the source case with its definition and period.
- A partner may be named only with its current relationship status.
- A platform feature may be included only if a participant or staff member uses it.
- A risk must be matched to a design choice, contingency, or evaluation question.
- A budget figure must be traceable to an activity quantity.
- A post-grant benefit must have an owner other than “the community” unless a specific community body is defined.
- Any sentence that could describe an unrelated project without alteration is too generic and must be revised.


## Measurement design

Define indicators before claiming outcomes.


| Level | Example question | Suitable indicator | Not sufficient alone |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Reach | Who actually participates? | Eligible enrollments and completion | Website visits |
| Delivery | Was the intervention delivered as designed? | Sessions delivered, attendance, facilitator coverage | Funds spent |
| Capability | What can participants do? | Pre-specified project or skill assessment | Self-reported enjoyment |
| Progression | What opportunity follows? | Education, paid work, or continued participation | Anecdotes without denominator |
| System | What structure changed? | Adopted pathway, recurring slots, changed access rules | Aspirational description |

For every indicator, specify baseline, target, measurement instrument, collection timing, and decision affected. A target without a baseline can still be used, but it must be labeled as a planning target rather than a forecast based on historical performance.

## Financial methodology

Build the request through four layers:

1. **Unit cost:** Cost per session, participant, site, license, or staff role.
2. **Operating volume:** Number of cohorts, sites, users, or months.
3. **Support costs:** Management, evaluation, accessibility, data, and administration required to deliver that volume.
4. **Contingency and sensitivity:** Costs affected by enrollment, facilities, staffing, or delayed adoption.

Then test three scenarios:

- **Constrained:** Essential pilot and evidence-building only.
- **Proposed:** Scale justified by delivery capacity.
- **Maximum:** Scale requiring additional confirmed capacity, not merely more money.

The selected scenario should explain why cheaper options fail and why larger options cannot yet be executed responsibly.

## Risk method

Score risks by their effect on the project logic, not by general seriousness.


| Risk category | Question | Example response |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Demand | Will intended participants enroll? | Pilot recruitment threshold before expansion |
| Delivery | Can staff deliver the model? | Training and maximum caseload |
| Mechanism | Will activities produce outcomes? | Staged evaluation before scaling |
| Adoption | Will institutions continue it? | Early adoption commitments or alternative owner |
| Technical | Will the platform support delivery? | Acceptance criteria and non-platform fallback |
| Financial | Will costs exceed assumptions? | Trigger for redesign or reduced cohort size |
| Control | Can the lead supervise funds and partners? | Approval rights and audit trail |

A risk without a decision trigger is only a description. For example, “review recruitment after three months” is operational only if it states what result causes continuation, revision, or termination.

## Collaboration workflow

Use short writing cycles with locked inputs.

1. **Scope lock:** Approve beneficiary, intervention, and change claim.
2. **Evidence lock:** Grade all claims and remove unsupported ones.
3. **Operations lock:** Approve delivery map and budget quantities.
4. **Narrative draft:** One writer produces the full prospectus.
5. **Fact review:** Owners verify only their claims.
6. **Independent review:** Unfamiliar readers test comprehension and scoring.
7. **Revision:** Change source documents first, then narrative.
8. **Freeze:** No new concepts after the designated freeze point.

During narrative drafting, contributors submit changes as proposed claims rather than rewritten paragraphs. This preserves a single voice and prevents contradictory promises.

## Quality thresholds

A section is ready only when all applicable answers are yes:

- Can a new reader restate it in two sentences?
- Are all numbers defined?
- Are assumptions visible?
- Does every activity have an owner?
- Does every cost have an output?
- Are San Francisco execution details specific?
- Could the claim survive scrutiny without oral explanation?
- Does the section answer its assigned question without repetition?

If fewer than all applicable answers are yes, continue analysis rather than polishing language.

## Production schedule

For a complete internal prospectus, use this sequence:

- **Days 1–2:** Beneficiary, problem, intervention, and platform role.
- **Days 3–4:** Theory of change, evidence grades, and measurement.
- **Days 5–6:** Delivery map, partners, and risk triggers.
- **Day 7:** Cost model and scenario selection.
- **Day 8:** Narrative draft from locked sources.
- **Day 9:** Independent and adversarial reviews.
- **Day 10:** Source corrections, final prose, and application conversion.

This schedule assumes the underlying program concept already exists. If beneficiary or intervention choices remain open, restart at the scope lock rather than drafting alternative narratives in parallel.

The strongest next work product is the intervention specification. It should describe one participant’s complete experience from first contact through the intended result, including who delivers each step and where FTSArcade is and is not essential. Once that is stable, the theory of change and budget can be built without circular revisions.

The remaining layer is operational governance for the writing effort itself: how decisions are made, how uncertainty is represented, and how the team prevents the prospectus from drifting away from what can actually be delivered. This is the control system around the methodology already established.

## Decision register

Every unresolved choice gets a record before drafting continues.


| Decision | Options | Decision rule | Needed information | Owner | Deadline | Consequence of delay |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Beneficiary | Define alternatives | Greatest demonstrated need and reachable population | Local evidence and delivery access | Program lead | Before scope lock | All downstream work pauses |
| Lead organization | Existing entity, sponsor, or partner | Legal eligibility and actual control | Entity documents and written confirmation | Authorized officer | Before submission | Application cannot be finalized |
| Funding scenario | Constrained, proposed, maximum | Delivery capacity and proportionality | Cost model | Budget owner | Before narrative | Request remains unsupported |
| Outcome claim | Narrow or broad result | Highest claim supportable at E1–E4 | Evidence register | Evidence reviewer | Before drafting | Narrative cannot assert impact |

A decision is closed only when its rationale is written. Reopening it requires new evidence or a material delivery constraint, not a stylistic preference.

## Uncertainty language

Use a controlled vocabulary so reviewers can distinguish plans from facts.


| Situation | Use | Avoid |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Established fact | “As of [date], [source] shows…” | “Everyone knows” |
| Team result | “In [period], the project produced…” | “Proven success” |
| External evidence | “Comparable evidence suggests…” | “Research proves our model” |
| Assumption | “The project will test whether…” | “This will inevitably” |
| Partnership under discussion | “The team is in discussion with…” | “Partner X will deliver” |
| Future action | “The lead will…” | “The community will naturally” |
| Target | “The planning target is…” | “The project guarantees” |

This language should survive editing. A later pass may shorten it, but it may not convert uncertainty into certainty.

## Number discipline

Create a data dictionary before using figures in the prospectus.


| Figure | Definition | Population | Period | Source | Calculation | Limitation |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Reach | People completing enrollment | Eligible San Francisco residents | Program year | Recruitment records | Count of accepted participants | Not website users |
| Completion | Participants meeting defined endpoint | Enrolled cohort | Cohort duration | Attendance system | Completers divided by starters | Define acceptable absence |
| Unit cost | Direct delivery cost per completer | Completed participants | Same cohort | Budget model | Eligible costs divided by completers | State excluded overhead |
| Outcome | Pre-specified result | Completers or enrolled cohort | Follow-up point | Assessment or verified record | Pre-registered method | Do not change after seeing results |

Every figure needs one definition. “Users,” “participants,” “young people,” and “beneficiaries” cannot be used interchangeably unless the data dictionary says they are identical.

## San Francisco specificity

A local execution claim should identify at least one of the following:

- A defined population or neighborhood
- A facility, institution, or service channel
- A local partner and its role
- A local cost condition
- A policy, procurement, or adoption pathway
- A barrier documented in San Francisco

If none can yet be named, the appropriate statement is that local presence remains to be established. The plan must then specify the sequence, responsible party, selection criteria, and decision point for establishing it.

Do not use local names decoratively. A named organization must have a role, status, and responsibility.

## Platform boundary

Write a boundary statement so FTSArcade does not absorb every project function.

Include four lists:

- **Essential functions:** Functions without which the intervention cannot operate.
- **Supporting functions:** Functions that improve delivery but have a fallback.
- **Excluded functions:** Activities humans or partners must perform, such as recruitment, supervision, assessment, or pastoral support.
- **Non-claims:** Outcomes the platform is not asserted to produce by itself.

For each essential function, document its current readiness, required development, acceptance test, responsible owner, and operating cost. A feature idea without an acceptance test remains outside the prospectus.

## Reviewer simulation

Have reviewers answer the prospectus in structured form rather than offering general impressions.


| Reviewer prompt | Passing response |
| :-- | :-- |
| Who changes, by how much, and how will that be known? | Names population, result, and measure |
| What must be true for the mechanism to work? | Identifies assumptions without inventing new ones |
| What can be delivered in the first year? | Separates first-year capacity from eventual scale |
| Where is San Francisco execution dependent on others? | Names dependencies and their status |
| Which claim is least supported? | Matches evidence register |
| What cost could be removed without changing outcomes? | Identifies a real efficiency or confirms proportionality |
| What survives if funding stops? | Names retained asset, owner, and operating need |

Reviewer comments become actions only when they identify missing information, ambiguity, or contradiction. Comments that merely request more inspiration do not justify expanding the scope.

## Contradiction audit

Before freezing the prospectus, test these pairs:

- Beneficiary count matches enrollment capacity.
- Outcome claims match evidence grades.
- Partner responsibilities match partner status.
- Budget quantities match delivery volume.
- Timeline matches staffing availability.
- Evaluation measures match promised outcomes.
- Platform functions match current or funded development.
- Post-grant owner matches governance arrangements.
- Requested funds match selected scenario.
- Lead-organization authority matches legal structure.

Any contradiction is resolved in the source case first. The prospectus should then be regenerated from the corrected source rather than patched independently.

## Page budget

Set a maximum length for the internal prospectus so additional analysis does not become uncontrolled prose.


| Section | Maximum length | Required display |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Decision summary | 1 page | Beneficiary, intervention, request, result |
| Need and beneficiaries | 2 pages | Baseline and source limitation |
| Intervention | 2 pages | Participant pathway |
| Mechanism and evidence | 3 pages | Causal table and evidence grades |
| Delivery | 2 pages | Responsibility and dependency table |
| Measurement | 1 page | Indicator table |
| Financial plan | 2 pages plus workbook | Scenario comparison |
| Durability and governance | 2 pages | Post-grant owner and controls |
| Risks | 1 page | Triggers and responses |

Tables count toward these limits. If a section exceeds its budget, prefer moving detail to an appendix over compressing it into ambiguous prose.

## Approval packet

The final internal packet should contain:

1. Approved source case
2. Decision register
3. Data dictionary
4. Evidence register
5. Delivery and budget workbooks
6. Internal prospectus
7. Contradiction audit
8. Application-conversion matrix
9. List of unresolved questions, each with owner and deadline

The signatory approves the packet only after confirming that no unresolved question affects eligibility, budget accuracy, or a central causal claim.

## Immediate production sequence

Start with these five artifacts, in order:

1. **Participant pathway:** First contact through final result.
2. **Platform boundary:** Essential, supporting, excluded, and non-claimed functions.
3. **Claim ledger:** No more than 25 claims, each graded and owned.
4. **First-year operating plan:** Only what current capacity can deliver.
5. **Unit-cost model:** Costs derived from that first-year plan.

Do not draft the full prospectus until those five agree with one another. Their agreement is the practical test that the methodology is working: the story, operations, evidence, and money describe the same project.

# more

The methodology can now be extended into the team’s day-to-day production rules: who may change the project, how revisions are accepted, and how the final prospectus remains auditable. This is less about adding sections and more about preventing silent changes as the deadline approaches.

## Change control

Any proposed edit falls into one of four classes.


| Change class | Examples | Required approval | Narrative effect |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Editorial | Grammar, sequence, shorter wording | Writer | None |
| Clarifying | Added definition, source, or limitation | Section owner | No new promise |
| Operational | New activity, partner, population, timeline, or cost | Program and budget owners | Update source case first |
| Foundational | New beneficiary, outcome, lead organization, or funding scenario | Decision owner and signatory | Reopen affected gates |

An editor may not convert a clarifying change into an operational promise. For example, naming a possible San Francisco venue does not authorize the prose to say the venue is secured.

## Revision record

Maintain a running record rather than relying on document comments.


| Date | Proposed change | Class | Reason | Evidence affected | Budget affected | Decision | Updated documents |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| [Date] | Narrow outcome from employment to completed projects | Foundational | No employment evidence | Yes | Yes | Accepted | Ledger, budget, prospectus |
| [Date] | Add named partner | Operational | Discussion began | No | No | Accepted with “in discussion” label | Delivery map |
| [Date] | Replace “may” with “will” | Editorial | Requested stronger tone | No | No | Rejected | None |

This record explains why the final prospectus says what it says. It also prevents deleted claims from reappearing during later compression.

## Meeting protocol

Use working sessions to close decisions, not to draft collectively.

Each meeting needs:

- One decision or blockage
- The source document under review
- Options already prepared
- Information missing
- Owner and deadline for the next action

A meeting output should be a decision, a rejected option, or a defined information request. Phrases such as “explore further” require a specific question, source, responsible person, and deadline; otherwise they are not actionable.

## Source hierarchy

When documents conflict, use this order:

1. Eligibility and legal constraints
2. Approved decision register
3. Budget and delivery workbook
4. Evidence register
5. Data dictionary
6. Internal prospectus
7. Application answers
8. Presentation or verbal explanation

Lower-ranked documents must be corrected to match higher-ranked ones. A polished application answer never overrides the budget or evidence register.

## Traceability matrix

Every prospectus paragraph receives an identifier and trace.


| Paragraph | Claim IDs | Figures | Operational dependencies | Evidence grades | Budget lines | Review status |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| P1 | C1, C2 | F1 | None | E2 | None | Fact-checked |
| P4 | C3, C4 | F2, F3 | Partner confirmation | E4 | B1 | Assumption labeled |
| P7 | C5 | F4 | Post-grant owner | E4 | B2 | Needs owner approval |

Before submission, every paragraph must be fact-checked, assumption-labeled, or removed. “Needs owner approval” is not an acceptable final status.

## Worked micro-example

The following illustrates the method using a hypothetical creative-technology project. It is not a proposed claim about FTSArcade and should not be copied into an application.

**Source claim:** Participants who attend facilitated weekly sessions will complete a small playable project.

**Evidence grade:** E4, testable assumption.

**Operational owner:** Program facilitator.

**Budget link:** Facilitator hours, venue, devices, and participant support.

**Acceptable prose:** “Each cohort will attend facilitated weekly sessions intended to result in a small playable project. Completion is a planning target, not a demonstrated outcome; the first cohorts will test whether this structure produces the expected completion rate.”

**Unacceptable prose:** “FTSArcade equips participants with industry-ready skills and creates transformative employment pathways.”

The unacceptable version adds skills, employment, industry readiness, and transformation without separate claims, evidence, owners, or costs.

## First-year boundary

The prospectus should distinguish three horizons without blending them.


| Horizon | May include | Must not include |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| First year | Capacity that named people and partners can deliver | Scale dependent on unsigned partners |
| Years two to five | Expansion triggered by stated results | Automatic growth without decision thresholds |
| After grant | Retained assets, adopted activities, or funded operations | Every activity continuing unchanged by default |

For each expansion trigger, state the metric, threshold, review date, and person authorized to approve expansion. Growth is therefore a decision, not an assumption.

## Language blacklist

Remove or rewrite these constructions unless a source explicitly supports them:

- “Revolutionary,” “world-class,” “unique,” or “cutting-edge”
- “Empower,” “unlock,” or “transform” without a defined change
- “Scalable” without a unit cost and constraint
- “Community-led” without a defined community authority
- “Sustainable” without a payer or operating model
- “Partners include” when discussions are incomplete
- “Data shows” when the source is an assumption
- “At scale” without a numerical scale
- “And more” after a list of activities
- “Solutions for San Francisco” without naming the population and condition

This is not a general style preference. These phrases conceal missing operational detail.

## Final audit sequence

Conduct the audits in this order so each one uses stable inputs:

1. **Eligibility audit:** Lead, authority, geography, registration, and charitable-use constraints.
2. **Logic audit:** Beneficiary, intervention, mechanism, and outcome agree.
3. **Evidence audit:** Every causal statement has a grade and limitation.
4. **Operations audit:** Capacity, timeline, and partner status support the plan.
5. **Financial audit:** Costs, scenarios, and request agree.
6. **Traceability audit:** Every paragraph maps to claims and figures.
7. **Compression audit:** Application answers preserve meaning within portal limits.
8. **Sign-off audit:** Unresolved items cannot affect a central claim.

No audit should be combined with copyediting. Correct facts first, then shorten, then polish.

## Team cadence

For the remaining preparation window, use short cycles:

- **Morning:** Update source documents and close one decision.
- **Midday:** Revise only paragraphs affected by that decision.
- **Afternoon:** Independent check of changed claims.
- **End of day:** Freeze accepted text and record rejected changes.

Limit each cycle to one foundational decision. Multiple simultaneous changes make contradictions difficult to detect and can invalidate earlier review work.

## Definition of done

The methodology is complete for the current stage when:

- One participant pathway is approved.
- The platform boundary is explicit.
- Every material claim has an owner, grade, and permitted wording.
- First-year output does not exceed named capacity.
- The budget reproduces the same outputs.
- Every expansion has a trigger.
- Every prospectus paragraph is traceable.
- Application answers are derived from frozen text.
- The signatory can identify the weakest assumption without assistance from the writer.

At that point, further effort should go into verification and submission mechanics, not into generating more vision language. The prospectus is finished when its claims are controlled, not when the team stops thinking of improvements.

## Drafter constraints

Give the drafter these written instructions:

- Use only facts in the source packet and documents it cites.
- Do not infer demand, outcomes, partnerships, capacity, ownership, or eligibility from the website.
- Label missing information explicitly.
- Use “planning target” for quantities not supported by operating history.
- Use relationship statuses exactly as supplied.
- Do not add a beneficiary, activity, partner, or outcome to make the proposal more compelling.
- Preserve limitations attached to evidence.
- Stop and request information when a required field is unknown.

The drafter may improve clarity and organization, but may not resolve project-design choices.

## Inference boundaries

State these prohibitions directly:


| Website observation | Permitted statement | Prohibited inference |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| A game is playable | The listed game is publicly accessible | It improves learning or employment |
| The arcade has several titles | Those titles are available | Users form a community or pipeline |
| A work-in-progress warning appears | The product remains under development | It is ready for citywide deployment |
| No account is required | Access has the stated technical condition | The target population can realistically use it |
| The site loads in a browser | Browser access is possible | San Francisco institutions will adopt it |
| The team built the site | The team has product-development capability | The team can deliver education or social services |

This prevents product facts from being silently promoted into impact claims.

## Missing-information protocol

For every unknown required field, the draft should insert a visible marker:

> **[INFORMATION NEEDED: responsible owner; why required; decision blocked]**

Examples:

- **[INFORMATION NEEDED: San Francisco delivery partner; required to support viability; blocks implementation claim]**
- **[INFORMATION NEEDED: facilitator unit cost; required to calculate request; blocks budget]**
- **[INFORMATION NEEDED: music-license scope; required to confirm permitted use; blocks rights claim]**

The marker stays until the owner supplies the fact. Deleting it without new information is a failed draft, even if the paragraph reads more smoothly.

## Source packet structure

Ask your team to complete ten short statements before drafting:

1. “The legal applicant is [entity], organized in [jurisdiction], with [tax status].”
2. “The project serves [included population] in [San Francisco geography], excluding [groups].”
3. “The current problem is [condition], affecting [quantity or unknown scale], based on [source].”
4. “A participant will [complete pathway], requiring [hours] over [period].”
5. “FTSArcade will provide [functions], but will not provide [human services].”
6. “First-year capacity is [cohorts], limited by [staff, facilities, recruitment, or budget].”
7. “The evidence for the mechanism is [grade and source], limited by [difference].”
8. “The request is [amount], calculated from [attached cost model].”
9. “After the grant, [named party] will maintain [asset or activity] at [annual cost].”
10. “The proposal must not claim [prohibited outcomes or relationships].”

If any statement cannot be completed, the proposal can still contain a placeholder, but it cannot be treated as finished.

## Acceptance test for a draft

Accept the first draft only if:

- Every factual sentence maps to the packet.
- Every unknown is visibly marked.
- No partner status has been upgraded.
- No website feature has acquired an unsupported outcome.
- The budget matches the stated cohort and staffing levels.
- The first-year plan fits stated capacity.
- The weakest assumption remains visible.
- A reviewer can distinguish facts, plans, and assumptions without asking the writer.

If these conditions fail, correct the draft or source packet. Do not compensate by adding more persuasive language.

## Recommended handoff

The practical handoff is therefore:

1. Completed ten-statement source packet
2. Evidence and budget attachments
3. Platform boundary and prohibited claims
4. Website address
5. Methodology and drafter constraints
6. Exact application questions and word limits, when available

That package is enough for a **controlled first draft with explicit gaps**. It becomes enough for a submission-ready proposal only after every information marker affecting eligibility, delivery, outcomes, rights, or cost has been resolved and verified.

# more

The handoff can be made reproducible by adding a coverage check, a draft-output format, and quantitative acceptance rules. This lets you determine whether the writer followed the source packet instead of judging the proposal by whether it sounds persuasive.

## Coverage matrix

Before drafting, assign every required proposal element one status.


| Element | Status | Meaning | Draft treatment |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Complete | All required fields and sources present | Writer may use it | Integrate normally |
| Partial | Core fact present, limitation missing | Writer may use only stated portion | Preserve limitation |
| Contested | Sources or owners disagree | No preferred version chosen | Show unresolved conflict |
| Unknown | Required fact absent | No inference permitted | Insert information marker |
| Prohibited | Team explicitly rejects claim | Cannot be used even if plausible | Omit |

A complete-looking proposal is not acceptable if any central element remains unknown or contested. Those statuses must remain visible.

## Required draft format

Ask for two outputs, not one polished document.

### Output A: annotated draft

Each paragraph ends with an internal trace:

> Source: C1, C4; Figures: F2; Evidence: E4; Status: assumption labeled.

Traces can be removed after review, but their presence makes unsupported additions easy to detect.

### Output B: exception report

The writer must separately list:

- Information markers and why each blocks a claim
- Source conflicts
- Claims considered but rejected
- Website observations intentionally not used
- Assumptions that materially affect viability, cost, or outcomes
- Any place where available evidence was too weak to answer an application criterion

If the exception report is empty on the first draft, treat that as a warning sign. A project at this stage should have unresolved issues.

## Scoring the draft

Use this review score before discussing writing quality.


| Test | Pass rule |
| :-- | :-- |
| Traceability | At least 95% of factual sentences map to a source ID |
| No silent inference | Zero unsupported website-derived claims |
| Status fidelity | 100% of partner and evidence statuses preserved |
| Gap visibility | 100% of unknown required fields marked |
| Numerical consistency | All figures match the data dictionary and budget |
| Capacity consistency | Planned volume does not exceed stated capacity |
| Prohibited claims | Zero prohibited claims appear |
| Criterion coverage | Every rubric criterion is answered or explicitly blocked |

A draft can be clearly written and still fail. It passes only after meeting every rule above.

## Paragraph acceptance

Review each paragraph with five yes-or-no questions:

1. Does the first sentence state its claim?
2. Can the claim be traced?
3. Is its certainty correctly labeled?
4. Does it add exactly one new idea?
5. Would deleting it remove useful information rather than tone?

Retain the paragraph only if all five answers are yes. Otherwise revise or delete it.

## Red-team protocol

Have one reviewer actively try to make the draft fail. Their task is not to improve tone but to find:

- A claim without a source
- A causal leap
- A partner whose role exceeds confirmation
- A number with no denominator or period
- A cost not tied to an activity
- A first-year promise requiring unsigned capacity
- A post-grant benefit without an owner
- A platform feature presented as a human service
- A limitation omitted from the narrative

Each finding is classified as:

- **Correct:** Fix the source or draft.
- **Already disclosed:** No action.
- **Requires project decision:** Return to decision register.
- **Speculative objection:** Record it, but do not expand the proposal merely to answer it.


## Final handoff checklist

The writer receives exactly these files:

1. Ten-statement source packet
2. Coverage matrix
3. Claim ledger and evidence grades
4. Data dictionary
5. Participant pathway
6. Platform boundary and prohibited claims
7. Delivery capacity and partner statuses
8. Budget workbook and selected scenario
9. Rights schedule
10. Decision and revision registers
11. Drafter constraints
12. Application questions and limits, if available
13. Website URL

The writer should not receive old pitches, informal brainstorming notes, or alternative visions unless they have been accepted in the decision register. Extra material creates opportunities for retired claims to re-enter the draft.

## Definition of a usable result

The resulting draft is usable when a reviewer can, without asking the writer:

- Trace every material claim
- Identify every blocking unknown
- Reproduce the budget from the stated activities
- Distinguish confirmed, discussed, and proposed relationships
- State the weakest assumption
- Determine whether the draft is ready, partially blocked, or not yet eligible

That is the correct standard. The website supplies product context, while this packet and its controls determine whether the resulting proposal is disciplined enough to review and eventually submit.

# more

The final layer is a small operating manual for the person managing the writer. It defines the assignment, the order of work, escalation rules, and the conditions under which drafting must stop. This prevents both unsupported invention and endless rewriting.

## Writer assignment

Give the writer this scope:

> Draft only what the approved source packet supports. Make gaps visible, preserve uncertainty, and do not improve the project by adding unstated activities, partners, outcomes, capacity, or evidence. Where a rubric criterion cannot be answered, say that it is blocked and identify the missing input.

The writer’s deliverables should be:

1. Annotated prospectus draft
2. Exception report
3. Traceability table
4. List of questions requiring a decision
5. Application-answer draft, only after the prospectus passes review

No slide deck, pitch rewrite, or alternative concept is part of the assignment unless separately approved.

## Work order

Require the writer to proceed in this order:

1. Read the prohibited claims and coverage matrix.
2. List every unknown or contested central element.
3. Draft the participant pathway and first-year plan.
4. Draft evidence, budget, and durability only from approved figures.
5. Draft the theory of change.
6. Write the summary last.
7. Add paragraph traces.
8. Run the scoring tests.
9. Submit the exception report with the draft.

This order ensures the summary cannot introduce a claim that the body has not accepted.

## Escalation rules

The writer must stop and request a decision when encountering:

- Two sources giving different figures
- A partner role stronger than its documented status
- A budget total that cannot be reproduced
- A required activity without an owner
- A claim graded E5 that appears essential
- Eligibility information that is missing or inconsistent
- Rights that do not cover the proposed use
- A first-year plan exceeding stated capacity
- An application question with no corresponding source material

The writer should not select the “most reasonable” resolution. The escalation note should quote the conflict, list the affected claims, and state which decision owner must resolve it.

## Question format

Every question returned to the team should use this format:

> **Decision needed:** [specific choice]
> **Why blocked:** [claim or section affected]
> **Options:** [only options supported by current material, plus “none approved”]
> **Consequence:** [what cannot be claimed until resolved]
> **Owner:** [one person]
> **Needed by:** [date]

This prevents broad questions such as “Can you clarify the impact?” which invite another round of vague vision writing.

## Review cycle

Use no more than three cycles.


| Cycle | Purpose | Permitted changes | Output |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Cycle 1 | Establish fidelity | Correct traces, gaps, and contradictions | Usable annotated draft |
| Cycle 2 | Resolve decisions | Incorporate newly approved source changes | Revised draft and updated registers |
| Cycle 3 | Compress and polish | Shorten without changing meaning | Submission-oriented text |

After Cycle 3, new ideas go into a future-version list rather than the current draft. This protects the deadline and the audit trail.

## Manager’s review checklist

Before accepting the draft, the manager checks:

- Writer did not add a beneficiary, partner, activity, or outcome.
- All central unknowns remain marked.
- Summary contains no claim absent from the body.
- Budget figures match the workbook exactly.
- Evidence language matches assigned grades.
- Prohibited claims are absent.
- Exception report explains every blocked rubric criterion.
- Questions request decisions rather than more prose.
- Application answers, if present, match frozen prospectus text.

If any check fails, return the draft with the failed rule identified. Do not give open-ended feedback such as “make it stronger.”

## Stop conditions

Stop drafting entirely when:

- The applicant is not confirmed eligible.
- Registration status prevents submission.
- No beneficiary or intervention has been approved.
- The budget cannot explain the request.
- Essential rights are unresolved.
- The team wants the writer to invent missing evidence or partners.

In those cases, the appropriate product is a gap report, not another proposal draft.

## Completed operating package

The full methodology now consists of:

- Source packet and coverage matrix
- Claim, evidence, decision, and revision registers
- Platform boundary and prohibited inferences
- Budget, capacity, and traceability controls
- Writer assignment, work order, and escalation rules
- Scoring tests and three-cycle review limit
- Stop conditions and manager checklist

That is sufficient to commission a **faithful first draft**. It is enough for a final proposal only after the team replaces every blocking information marker with verified source material and approves the resulting changes through the decision register.

# more

The package can be completed with a one-page operating charter and a set of acceptance records. These make the methodology enforceable: every person can see what they may decide, what the writer must return, and when the draft is ready to move forward.

## Operating charter

**Purpose:** Produce a faithful, reviewable proposal from approved sources without inventing missing facts.

**Authoritative inputs:** The approved source packet, registers, budget, rights schedule, and decision record override the website, prior pitches, and conversation.

**Writer authority:** The writer may organize, clarify, shorten, and identify gaps. The writer may not create project substance.

**Team authority:** Owners supply and approve facts in their areas. Only the designated decision owner may close a foundational choice.

**Conflict rule:** When sources conflict, drafting pauses for that claim. No one silently chooses the more compelling version.

**Gap rule:** Unknown required information remains visibly marked until a source is supplied.

**Change rule:** New ideas enter through the decision register before entering the prospectus.

**Finish rule:** A polished narrative is not complete unless its claims, numbers, responsibilities, and costs trace to approved sources.

## Role cards

| Role | Sole authority | Must supply | Cannot delegate |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Decision owner | Foundational choices | Written rationale | Beneficiary, lead, outcome, funding scenario |
| Program owner | Intervention and delivery | Pathway, capacity, partner status | Unconfirmed commitments |
| Evidence owner | Evidence grades | Sources and limitations | Upgrading certainty |
| Budget owner | Costs and scenarios | Reproducible calculations | Narrative-only figures |
| Rights owner | Asset permissions | Ownership and restrictions | Assumed licenses |
| Writer | Presentation | Traces and exception report | Project decisions |
| Manager | Process acceptance | Review record | Overriding subject owners |
| Signatory | Final submission | Approval statement | Signing unresolved central gaps |

One person may hold multiple roles, but the record should still identify which role approved each item.

## Acceptance records

Use four short records rather than informal approval messages.

### Source acceptance

> I approve [document/version] as source material for [scope]. It contains [number] unresolved items, none of which affect [or do affect] central claims.

### Decision acceptance

> I select [option] because [reason]. Options rejected: [options]. Documents requiring revision: [documents].

### Draft acceptance

> Cycle [number] passes the manager checklist. Exceptions remain in [sections]. New ideas deferred: [list].

Use consistent labels so reviewers know what they are reading.


| Label | Meaning | Permitted use |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| S0 | Incomplete source packet | Gap identification only |

| D3 | Compressed and polished | Application preparation |
| F | Frozen submission version | Submission only |
| X | Rejected or superseded | Historical reference only |

No file should circulate without a label and date. Superseded files remain available for audit but cannot be mined for new claims.

Use this instruction with the writer:

> Using only the attached S1 sources, produce an annotated prospectus, traceability table, and exception report. Do not infer missing facts from the website. Preserve all limitations and relationship statuses. If a central element is unknown, contested, or prohibited, mark it and explain the blocked claim. Return questions in the required decision format. Do not draft final application answers until D1 passes review.

That message, together with the charter and source package, is the complete writing brief.

## Final readiness states

| State | Definition | Next action |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Not ready | Beneficiary, intervention, eligibility, or budget absent | Complete source packet |
| Draftable | S1 sources approved, gaps visible | Commission D1 |
| Reviewable | D1 traces and exception report complete | Run audits |
| Revisable | Decisions returned and approved | Produce D2 |
| Compressible | Claims stable and contradictions resolved | Produce D3 |
| Submittable | F version approved and portal answers verified | Submit |
| Blocked | Eligibility, rights, or central evidence unresolved | Stop and obtain decision |

The methodology is now sufficient to govern the work from source collection through submission. It does not replace the missing project facts: it tells the team exactly when those facts are adequate, how a writer may use them, and when drafting must pause rather than conceal a gap.

The current description does not support claims that the project will:

- Revitalize old buildings
- Increase running or use of public routes
- Popularize San Francisco
- Produce economic development
- Benefit participating businesses equitably

Those may become research questions or separately funded commercial activities. They should not appear as promised outcomes unless each has a beneficiary, mechanism, evidence grade, delivery owner, and budget.

For that framing, the proposal would need to explain:

- Which residents or visitors benefit, and how
- Which buildings, routes, or landmarks are included
- What permission their owners or responsible authorities have given
- Why featuring them changes conditions rather than merely advertising them
- How much access remains free
- Who controls advertising standards and business selection
- Why commercial revenues do not become the project’s real purpose

# businesses are the ones upselling while the city is getting popularity and notoriety through its business

That can be the business model, but it does not by itself establish the charitable project. Businesses upselling their products and San Francisco gaining exposure are commercial and promotional effects; the grant still needs a defined public benefit that would justify charitable funding under the competition rules.[^22_1]

The chain would need to be stated like this:

> Participating businesses fund or host public arcade installations. The installations feature approved San Francisco places and businesses. Public use creates measurable cultural, educational, recreational, or economic benefits for San Francisco residents. Advertising and upselling remain subordinate to that public purpose.

Every arrow in that chain is currently unproven and must be labeled as an assumption.

## What needs definition

- **Public beneficiary:** Residents, workers, visitors, specific neighborhoods, or another defined group.
- **Public result:** More than exposure—such as free cultural access, documented local history, youth activity, or verified spending in underserved areas.
- **Business benefit:** What businesses receive and why that benefit is incidental rather than the project’s purpose.
- **Selection rules:** How businesses and landmarks are chosen without favoring insiders or paying advertisers unfairly.
- **Free access:** What the public can use without buying anything.
- **Evidence:** Why promotion and upselling should produce the stated city benefit.

“Popularity” and “notoriety” are not yet measurable outcomes. A reviewable claim would specify who becomes aware of what, through what use, and why that awareness benefits San Francisco residents.

Thus, the proposal can include both commerce and civic promotion, but commerce must fund or enable a specified public result. It cannot be the result itself.

# what are more resources that are active in the current environment / eco-system that have the words we can provide in our statements / pages / mission statement / company dedication etc.

The most useful language comes from active San Francisco cultural, small-business, parks, and equity programs. These phrases can show alignment, but they should be cited or paraphrased accurately—not copied into your mission as though they describe results you already deliver.

## Strongest language sources

| Resource | Language you can responsibly adapt | Best use |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| [San Francisco Arts Commission](https://www.sf.gov/arts-commission-mission-values) | “Arts essential to daily life,” “enlivening the urban environment,” “cultural equity and access” [^23_1] | Public art, cultural access, and neighborhood installations |
| [Cultural Districts Program](https://www.sf.gov/san-francisco-cultural-districts-program) | “Cultural and economic vitality,” “preserve, strengthen, and promote cultural assets,” “live, work, and prosper” [^23_2][^23_3] | Neighborhood culture and community-defined place promotion |
| [Office of Economic and Workforce Development](https://www.sf.gov/departments--office-economic-and-workforce-development) | “Thriving and resilient economy,” “shared prosperity,” “barriers to opportunity removed” [^23_4] | Small-business participation and economic inclusion |
| [Office of Small Business](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/OSB_FY24-25_Annual_Report.pdf) | “Support, preserve, and protect small businesses” and champion the city’s diverse business community [^23_5] | Business-hosting and corridor participation |
| [SF Planning Equity Plan](https://sfplanning.org/project/racial-and-social-equity-action-plan) | “Inclusive, sustainable, and prosperous neighborhoods” and “lasting resilience” [^23_6] | Equity commitments, if backed by specific actions |
| [Recreation and Park Department](https://sfrecpark.org/419/Who-We-Are) | “Enriching recreational activities” for “the well-being of everyone in our diverse community” [^23_7] | Public play, recreation, and access |
| [SF Travel](https://www.sftravel.com/about-sf-travel) | “Top global destination,” “innovation and sustainability,” and welcoming, accessible experiences [^23_8] | Destination promotion, not resident benefit alone |

## Phrases suitable for a mission

These are concise adaptations, not official endorsements:

- “Free cultural and recreational access in everyday public places.”
- “Shared prosperity for San Francisco residents, workers, and neighborhood businesses.”
- “Preserve and promote neighborhood cultural assets.”
- “Support resilient neighborhood commercial corridors.”
- “Connect public play with local places, history, and small businesses.”
- “Make San Francisco’s cultural life visible and accessible to all communities.”
- “Build lasting community resilience through locally rooted creative experiences.”


## Use with care

“Equitable,” “community-led,” and “revitalization” carry specific meanings in San Francisco policy. The Cultural Districts Program, for example, emphasizes stabilizing vulnerable communities and protecting them from displacement, not simply increasing a neighborhood’s popularity.[^23_3]

Before using equity or stabilization language, the project should identify:

- Which communities define the cultural story
- Who gives permission to feature a landmark, route, or business
- How benefits reach existing residents and businesses
- How advertising and upselling remain subordinate to public access
- How the project avoids contributing to displacement or excluding nonpaying users

A careful mission formulation would be: “We create accessible cultural and recreational installations that celebrate San Francisco’s neighborhoods and support its small businesses, while keeping public participation free and community permission central.” That aligns with the city language without claiming that popularity alone constitutes a public benefit.
